# CLICK BLOCKS -- ANGLE SELECTION, UGC SCRIPT & DISSECTION

---

## PART 1: THE WINNING ANGLE

### The Angle: "Nothing competes with the screen -- until now"

**The pain point:** Parents are trapped in a daily losing battle against screens. They KNOW screen time is bad. Every article, every paediatrician, every school newsletter tells them. But they can't find a single toy that actually holds their kid's attention the way a screen does. So the screens win. Every single day. And every single day, the guilt gets heavier.

This is not a knowledge problem. It's a helplessness problem. They already have the desire to fix it -- it burns in them every time they see their kid with glazed eyes scrolling on the iPad. What they don't have is a solution that actually WORKS.

### Why this angle is the strongest

**1. The desire already exists at massive scale.** Per Schwartz's cardinal rule: copy does not create desire. It channels desire that already exists. Screen time guilt is arguably THE dominant parenting anxiety of the 2020s. Every parent scrolling Facebook or Instagram right now feels it. That means our addressable audience is essentially every parent on the platform.

**2. It's emotionally charged enough to stop a scroll.** This isn't a nice-to-have improvement. This is a daily source of guilt, conflict, and worry about their child's development. High emotional charge = high scroll-stopping potential. Parents don't casually think about screen time. They FEEL it -- in the pit of their stomach when they hand over the iPad to buy themselves 20 minutes of peace.

**3. The product has a genuine, ownable mechanism to solve it.** Click Blocks doesn't just "compete" with screens through novelty. It uses the screen's own weapon -- dopamine micro-rewards via the magnetic click sound -- to hold attention the same way. This is a Stage 3 sophistication play: a NEW MECHANISM that makes an old promise work. We're not saying "buy a toy to reduce screen time" (every toy claims this). We're saying "this is the first toy that hijacks the same dopamine loop the screens use."

**4. The proof already exists in customer language.** Review after review says the same thing in different words: "He didn't even mention his tablet." "She doesn't think about any electrical device." "Kept him amused all afternoon -- didn't even mention his tablet." We don't have to manufacture the transformation story. Real parents are already telling it.

**5. It works for cold traffic at scale.** Unlike the ADHD angle (too niche) or the sibling bonding angle (too soft), screen time guilt hits EVERY parent. A mum with one kid feels it. A dad with four feels it. A grandparent watching their grandchild zombied out on YouTube feels it. It scales.

### Angles considered but rejected

| Angle | Why rejected |
|---|---|
| **Toys collecting dust** | Good supporting point but not emotionally urgent enough to stop a scroll on its own. It's frustrating, not gut-wrenching. Works better as a bridge/objection handler ("I know, you've bought toys before that ended up in the cupboard...") than as the lead. |
| **ADHD/neurodivergent engagement** | Powerful for the parents who live it, but too narrow for a cold traffic scaling ad. This is a retargeting angle or a separate ad for a specific audience segment -- not the first test. |
| **Loss of imagination/creativity** | Too abstract. "My kid isn't creative enough" isn't a feeling parents wake up with at 3am. "My kid won't put the iPad down" IS. Creativity is a supporting benefit, not a scroll-stopper. |
| **Sibling conflict** | Real pain but not unique to screens. Siblings fought before iPads existed. It doesn't tie tightly enough to the product's core mechanism (dopamine click). Better as a secondary mention. |

## PART 2: THE UGC SCRIPT (Up to 60 seconds)

**Format:** UGC-style. Mum talking to camera, casual, in her kitchen or living room. Feels like she's telling a friend, not performing an ad. Natural lighting, phone-shot aesthetic.

**Awareness target:** Problem-Aware parents
**Flow:** Problem > Solution > Product > Offer

---

### THE SCRIPT

**[0-3s] HOOK**

*(Mum looking into camera, slightly exasperated, genuine face)*

**"I used to hand my kid the iPad just to get 20 minutes of peace."**

*(On screen text: "I used to hand my kid the iPad...")*

---

**[3-8s] BRIDGE**

*(Mum now talking more directly, slight guilt in her voice, still in kitchen)*

**"And every single time, I'd feel that little stab of guilt. Like... I know it's not great for him. But nothing else held his attention for more than five minutes."**

---

**[8-15s] PROBLEM INTENSIFICATION**

*(Cut to B-roll: kid on couch, glazed eyes, scrolling iPad. Cut back to mum.)*

**"I'd buy toys. Nice ones. And they'd sit in a cupboard after day one. Because nothing can compete with a screen... or so I thought."**

---

**[15-30s] SOLUTION & MECHANISM (The Hold)**

*(Mum picks up Click Blocks from counter, holds them up. Cut to kid building with them at the table, focused, engaged.)*

**"Then someone told me about these. And I'll be honest, I almost didn't bother. Another toy, right? But here's what's different -- every time the pieces click together, they make this little sound."**

*(Cut to close-up: blocks snapping together -- the satisfying CLICK)*

**"And that sound gives them a tiny hit of dopamine. The same thing the screens use to keep them hooked. Except now it's keeping them building. My son sat there for two hours. TWO HOURS. Without asking for the iPad once."**

---

**[30-45s] PROOF & BENEFIT (Show Don't Tell)**

*(Cut to kid proudly showing mum what he built -- a castle/world. Mum's face genuinely impressed. Cut to kid carrying creation to the car.)*

**"Now he builds these little worlds before school. Carries them to the car. His teacher told me his focus has actually improved. And I'm not fighting him for the iPad anymore."**

*(On screen text: "82% of parents report less screen time")*

---

**[45-55s] CTA**

*(Mum back to camera, warm, direct.)*

**"They're called Click Blocks. Right now they're doing a summer sale -- the big set is like twenty-four bucks. And there's a 30-day guarantee so if your kid doesn't love them, you get your money back. But honestly? Good luck getting them back."**

*(On screen text: "SUMMER SALE -- Up to 73% OFF" + "30-Day Guarantee")*

*(Cut to: kid building, happy, no screen in sight. Product shot with pricing.)*

---

**[55-60s] CLOSE**

**"Link's in the ad."**

*(End card: Click Blocks logo + "Shop Now")*

---

**Total runtime:** ~55-60 seconds

## PART 3: SCRIPT DISSECTION -- WHY EVERY LINE IS WHERE IT IS

### Line-by-line psychology:

---

**"I used to hand my kid the iPad just to get 20 minutes of peace."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

This is the hook. It has 1-2 seconds to stop the scroll. Here's what it does:

- **Calls out the ideal audience instantly.** Only a parent would say this. Only a parent would feel the weight of this sentence. Every non-parent scrolls past. Every parent stops. This is Hopkins' headline principle: "pick out people you can interest."
- **Implies a benefit through subtext.** The word "used to" does all the heavy lifting. She USED TO do this. Past tense. Something changed. The benefit is implied without being stated: she found something better. That's a curiosity gap.
- **Drives curiosity.** What changed? Why doesn't she do it anymore? The viewer needs to know. Slippery slope begins.
- **Removes shame by normalising.** She's not judging. She's confessing. She did the same thing. This triggers identification -- "That's me. I do that." Per Schwartz's identification technique, the viewer sees themselves in the ad before any selling begins.
- **It's only 14 words.** Scannable. Not a wall of text. Fits two lines on mobile. Per the Evolve system: looks like a headline, not a paragraph.

**Big 4 analysis:** SAFE (normalises guilt, "I did it too"), USEFUL (implies a solution exists). The hook doesn't need all four. Two hit hard enough.

---

**"And every single time, I'd feel that little stab of guilt."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

This is the bridge opening. It INTENSIFIES the desire by naming the emotion the parent feels but rarely says out loud. "Little stab of guilt" is specific and visceral -- not "I felt bad" (generic). Per Hopkins' specificity principle, specific language is believed; vague language is ignored.

This line also does something subtle: it keeps the mum as the protagonist, not the product. We're still in HER story. Per Rule #1 of the Evolve Copywriting 101: nobody cares about your product. They care about themselves.

---

**"Like... I know it's not great for him. But nothing else held his attention for more than five minutes."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

Two things happening:

1. **Validates the objection before it's raised.** The parent watching is already thinking "yeah but I HAVE to give them the screen because nothing else works." This line says: I know. Me too. It pre-empts the defence mechanism. Per Schwartz's Technique 4 (Redefinition), we're redefining the parent's failure as a product gap, not a personal flaw.

2. **Creates the open loop.** "Nothing else held his attention" -- the viewer is now thinking: "but clearly SOMETHING did, because she said 'used to'..." They need to keep watching to find out what.

The "Like..." pause at the start is deliberate. It sounds like real speech. Like she's searching for the words. Per Rule #3: write how people talk, not how they read.

---

**"I'd buy toys. Nice ones. And they'd sit in a cupboard after day one."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

This addresses the #2 objection before we introduce the product: "I've tried toys before. They don't work." Per the Objection > Claim > Proof > Benefit cycle, we surface the objection FIRST so we can knock it down.

"Nice ones" is doing work -- it communicates "I spent real money and it still didn't work." The parent watching has a cupboard full of $40 toys proving this exact point. The specificity of "cupboard after day one" makes it vivid and relatable.

---

**"Because nothing can compete with a screen... or so I thought."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

This is the PIVOT. The Slippery Slope hinge point. Everything before this was problem intensification. Everything after is solution.

"Or so I thought" is one of the most powerful open-loop phrases in direct response. It signals: everything you believed is about to be challenged. The viewer cannot stop watching now. Their brain needs the resolution.

Per Schwartz's Gradualization technique: we've built a chain of accepted statements (I handed him the iPad, I felt guilty, nothing else worked, toys collected dust). Each one was immediately accepted because it's true. Now we pivot -- and the viewer follows because they've been saying "yes" to every statement so far.

---

**"Then someone told me about these."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

Third-party discovery framing. She didn't find these through an ad (even though the viewer IS watching an ad). She heard about them from someone else. This is Schwartz's Camouflage technique -- borrowing believability from a trusted source (word of mouth > advertising).

"These" -- not "Click Blocks." Not yet. We don't name the product until after we've built desire. Per Rule #1: nobody cares about your product. They care about results.

---

**"And I'll be honest, I almost didn't bother. Another toy, right?"**

*Why this line. Why here.*

This is genius-level objection handling disguised as authenticity. The viewer is ALREADY thinking "yeah, another toy that won't work." By having the mum say it first, we:

1. Prove we understand their skepticism (SAFE signal)
2. Neutralise the objection before it calcifies
3. Create another curiosity gap ("she almost didn't bother... but she did... and something happened")

Per Masterson's Great Leads: the Story Lead lets you sneak into the psyche sideways, delivering proof before the reader realises what you're doing.

---

**"But here's what's different -- every time the pieces click together, they make this little sound."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

THIS is the mechanism introduction. Per Schwartz's Stage 3 sophistication strategy: introduce a NEW MECHANISM that makes the old promise work. We are not saying "it's a great toy" (Stage 1 claim). We are not saying "it's the BEST toy" (Stage 2 enlargement). We are saying: here's HOW it works differently.

"Here's what's different" is the transition phrase. It tells the viewer: pay attention, this is the part that matters.

Notice we don't say "magnetic building blocks" -- that would categorize the product (categorization = death per Evolve). We describe WHAT IT DOES (makes a clicking sound), not WHAT IT IS (magnetic blocks).

---

**"And that sound gives them a tiny hit of dopamine. The same thing the screens use to keep them hooked."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

This is the Reason-Why. Per Hopkins: every claim needs a reason. We didn't just say "it holds their attention." We explained WHY it holds their attention -- with a mechanism the parent already understands (dopamine = the thing making screens addictive).

This reframes the enemy's weapon as OUR weapon. The screens use dopamine against parents. Now the parents have a tool that uses dopamine FOR them. This is new hope through mechanism, not just another toy claim.

"Tiny hit of dopamine" -- specific, scientific-sounding but 6th-grade language. Not jargon. A mum would actually say this.

---

**"Except now it's keeping them building."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

The payoff. Short. Punchy. The dopamine that was keeping them scrolling is now keeping them building. One sentence flips the entire problem on its head. This is Schwartz's Redefinition in one line -- the enemy becomes the ally.

---

**"My son sat there for two hours. TWO HOURS. Without asking for the iPad once."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

PROOF through specificity. Per Hopkins: "two hours" is believed. "A long time" is not. The repetition ("two hours. TWO HOURS.") mimics how a parent would actually say this -- with disbelief and emphasis. It's not written copy. It's spoken language captured.

"Without asking for the iPad once" -- this is the SHOW DON'T TELL. We didn't say "it reduces screen time." We SHOWED what reduced screen time looks like in real behaviour. Per Evolve Strategy #4: showing triggers the emotional brain (belief). Telling triggers the logical brain (skepticism).

---

**"Now he builds these little worlds before school. Carries them to the car."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

Behavioural transformation shown, not told. This is lifted almost directly from a real customer review ("They often carry their creations to the car in the morning"). We're using real customer behaviour as proof because it's more believable than any claim we could manufacture.

"Little worlds" -- not "structures" or "creations." Little worlds. This implies imagination, storytelling, depth. It communicates the educational/creative benefit without SAYING "educational" or "creative" (telling = selling death).

---

**"His teacher told me his focus has actually improved."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

Authority proof via third party. A teacher said it -- not the brand, not the mum hoping, not an ad. A teacher. This is Schwartz's Camouflage technique again: borrowing conviction from a source the viewer already trusts.

"Actually improved" -- the word "actually" communicates surprise. Even the mum didn't expect this. That surprise makes it more believable than a confident claim would.

---

**"And I'm not fighting him for the iPad anymore."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

Loop closure. The hook opened with the iPad battle. This line closes it. The problem stated in second one is now resolved. The viewer's brain gets the satisfying resolution it's been chasing since the hook.

This is also the ultimate BENEFIT -- shown through the absence of pain, not the presence of a feature. She didn't say "Click Blocks improved our family life." She said "I'm not fighting him anymore." Specific. Behavioural. Real.

---

**"They're called Click Blocks."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

Product name appears for the first time at the 45-second mark. We waited THIS long on purpose. Per Rule #1: nobody cares about your product. They care about themselves. We earned the right to name the product by telling a story that was about THEM first.

---

**"Right now they're doing a summer sale -- the big set is like twenty-four bucks."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

Price anchoring through casual language. "Like twenty-four bucks" sounds like a friend telling you about a deal, not an ad reading a price tag. The "summer sale" creates genuine urgency (seasonal, not fake scarcity).

$24 for a toy that gave her kid two hours of screen-free play? That's less than a month of a streaming subscription. The value reframe happens automatically in the parent's head.

---

**"And there's a 30-day guarantee so if your kid doesn't love them, you get your money back."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

Risk reversal. Per the Evolve CTA rules: SAFE signal. Removes the last objection ("what if it doesn't work for MY kid?"). 30 days is long enough to feel safe but specific enough to be believable.

---

**"But honestly? Good luck getting them back."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

Pattern interrupt in the guarantee. Per Evolve's CTA section: "New and novel isn't just for your headline -- it applies to your offer too." This line is funny, confident, and it's actually a stealth social proof claim: this product is so good your kid won't give it back. It ends the CTA on a smile, not a hard sell.

---

**"Link's in the ad."**

*Why this line. Why here.*

Clean. Direct. No friction. Three words. The simplest possible CTA instruction. The entire script has been a slippery slope leading to this moment. She's not begging. She's not pushing. She's just pointing.

## PART 4: THE HOOK -- FIRST 3 SECONDS

### "I used to hand my kid the iPad just to get 20 minutes of peace."

**What it does in the first 3 seconds:**

**1. AUDIENCE SELECTION (Hopkins' Headline Principle)**
This sentence is a filter. It instantly selects parents and repels everyone else. A 22-year-old without kids scrolls past. A 35-year-old mum with a 6-year-old stops dead. The hook's job is not to appeal to everyone -- it's to pick out the people you can interest, and ONLY those people.

**2. PATTERN INTERRUPT THROUGH CONFESSION**
Facebook and Instagram feeds are full of polished, aspirational content. A mum confessing she uses the iPad as a babysitter breaks the pattern. It's unexpected honesty. The thumb stops because this doesn't look or feel like an ad -- it feels like a real person admitting something real.

**3. CREATES AN IRRESISTIBLE CURIOSITY GAP**
The past tense "used to" is the scroll-stopper. If she said "I hand my kid the iPad" -- that's a statement. Relatable but complete. There's no reason to keep watching. But "I USED TO" -- that implies she stopped. Something changed. The viewer's brain NEEDS to know what replaced the iPad. This is the Slippery Slope from the very first second.

**4. TRIGGERS IDENTIFICATION (Schwartz Technique #2)**
The viewer doesn't just understand this sentence -- they FEEL it. They've done the exact same thing. Probably today. The hook creates instant identification: "this person is me." Once that bond forms, the viewer is emotionally invested in the story. They're not watching an ad anymore. They're watching their own experience reflected back at them.

**5. IMPLIES A BENEFIT WITHOUT STATING ONE**
No benefit is mentioned. No product is mentioned. No claim is made. Yet the viewer already senses a benefit exists -- because "used to" promises a before-and-after. The benefit lives entirely in subtext. Per the Evolve core operating principle: "It's not what you say, it's what you communicate."

**Why it will stop the scroll:**
Because it's not trying to. It's not screaming "50% OFF!" or "BEST TOY EVER!" It's a quiet confession that 82% of parents on the platform have lived. The most powerful scroll-stoppers don't demand attention -- they earn it by saying the thing the viewer has been thinking but hasn't said out loud.

The hook passes all three Golden Rules:
- **Calls out the ideal audience:** Only parents relate
- **Implies a benefit:** "Used to" = something better exists
- **Drives curiosity:** What changed? Keep watching

It passes the Four U's:
- **UNIQUE:** Confessional tone, not a typical ad hook
- **USEFUL:** Directly addresses their #1 daily struggle
- **URGENT:** They're living this problem right now, today
- **ULTRA-SPECIFIC:** "20 minutes of peace" -- not "some quiet time"
