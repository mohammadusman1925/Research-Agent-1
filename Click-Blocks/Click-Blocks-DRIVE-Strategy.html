Part 1

Discover

Understanding what the market already wants — not what we want to tell them. Who's buying, what stage they're at, and what emotional triggers drive their decisions.

1.1 Mass Desire Analysis

The mass desire is not "magnetic building blocks." The mass desire is: "I want my kid to put the screen down and be a kid again — without me having to fight for it."

This desire predates Click Blocks. It's the same desire that drives parents to buy educational toys, enforce screen-time rules, sign kids up for activities, and lie awake at 2am wondering if their child's brain is being rewired by YouTube Shorts. Click Blocks intercepts this desire at the moment the parent has exhausted willpower and is searching for a solution that doesn't require constant supervision.

Desire dimensionAssessmentEvidence
UrgencyHIGHThe guilt is daily. Every handed-over iPad is a fresh reminder. Not a "someday" problem — it's happening right now, today, every day.
Staying powerHIGHScreen time anxiety doesn't resolve itself. It grows as children age and devices multiply. The desire renews every school morning, every dinner table, every bedtime.
ScopeHIGHEvery parent on social media is addressable. Screen guilt crosses income, geography, parenting style. Universal anxiety in the 2020s.

Forces amplifying this desire:

  • Schools sending home screen-time warning newsletters
  • Paediatricians flagging attention span and social development concerns
  • Social media showing "perfect" screen-free households (comparison guilt)
  • Rising ADHD diagnosis rates linked to excessive screen use in parenting discourse
  • Parents' own awareness of their own phone addiction projected onto fears for their kids

1.4 ICP Profiles

ICP 1: The Guilty Scroller (Primary)

Trigger event: Caught herself handing over the iPad for the third time today. Saw a post about screen time damage and felt sick. Or: school sent home a newsletter about device limits and she felt called out.

Mental state: "I know it's bad. I keep saying 'just 20 more minutes' and it turns into two hours. I'm a terrible parent."

What she's tried: Screen-time limits (meltdowns). Taking devices away (tantrums). Buying toys (cupboard after day one). Activity books (10 minutes max).

Awareness: Problem-Aware. Knows screens are bad. Doesn't know a toy can fight back using dopamine.

Sophistication: Stage 3 — needs a new mechanism.

Dominant trigger: Freedom from guilt.

Belief blocking the sale: "No toy can compete with a screen. I've tried. They all end up in the cupboard."

Emotional doorway: Guilt → Relief.

ICP 2: The Developmental Worrier (Primary)

Trigger event: Noticed her child can't sit still without a device. Teacher mentioned attention issues. Or: read an article linking screen time to ADHD-like symptoms and panicked.

Mental state: "His attention span is shot. He can't focus on anything that isn't a screen. What have I done?"

What she's tried: More outdoor time (weather/logistics). Reading time (he loses focus). Educational apps (still a screen).

Awareness: Problem-Aware, edging Solution-Aware. Actively searching for "toys that improve focus."

Sophistication: Stage 2–3.

Dominant trigger: Protection of child's development.

Belief blocking the sale: "The damage might already be done. A toy can't undo years of screen time."

Emotional doorway: Fear → Hope.

ICP 3: The Toy Graveyard Parent (Secondary)

Trigger event: Just cleaned out the playroom. Found $300 worth of toys her kids played with once. Swore she wouldn't buy another toy that collects dust.

Mental state: "I'm done wasting money on toys they don't use. Unless someone can prove this one is different."

What she's tried: Expensive educational toys, LEGO sets (completed and shelved), arts & crafts (mess, supervision).

Awareness: Solution-Aware. Knows toys exist. Doesn't believe any will work.

Sophistication: Stage 3–4. Very sceptical. Needs proof.

Dominant trigger: Superiority ("I'm a smart buyer, show me why this is different").

Belief blocking the sale: "Every toy company says their product is different. None of them are."

Emotional doorway: Cynicism → Surprise.

ICP 4: The ADHD/Neurodiverse Parent (Segment)

Trigger event: Searching for sensory toys or focus aids for a neurodivergent child. Exhausted from trying to find something that holds attention beyond 5 minutes.

Mental state: "Nothing holds his attention. Not toys, not books, not activities. Only the screen works and I hate it."

What she's tried: Sensory toys (fidget spinners lasted a week). Structured activities (overwhelmed by instructions). OT-recommended tools (expensive, clinical).

Awareness: Solution-Aware. Actively searching.

Sophistication: Stage 3. Wants to know WHY it works for her specific child.

Dominant trigger: Care and protection.

Belief blocking the sale: "This isn't designed for kids like mine."

Emotional doorway: Exhaustion → Discovery.

ICP 5: The Gift-Giver (Seasonal)

Trigger event: Birthday or Christmas approaching. Wants to buy something meaningful, not more junk. Or: grandparent who sees the grandkids glued to screens and wants to intervene.

Mental state: "I want to give them something they'll actually use. Something that makes me look like a thoughtful gift-giver, not just another Amazon order."

Awareness: Unaware to Problem-Aware. May not feel the screen guilt directly but witnesses it.

Sophistication: Stage 2. A strong claim with social proof is enough.

Dominant trigger: Social approval + care for loved ones.

Belief blocking the sale: "Will they actually play with this or will it sit in the pile?"

Emotional doorway: Uncertainty → Confidence.

Part 2

Reframe

Changing what the customer believes. Most parents have a wrong idea about why their kid can't put the screen down and what will fix it. Our job: show the real cause, explain why everything else failed, reveal why this works.

2.1 Problem Mechanism

LayerDetail
Surface problem"My kid won't put the screen down. I've tried taking it away and it's World War 3."
True causeScreens aren't winning because they're better than toys. They're winning because they deliver constant micro-dopamine rewards — likes, scrolls, level-ups, new content every 15 seconds. Every toy competes against this dopamine drip and loses because toys don't have a reward loop built in. The child isn't choosing the screen. The screen's chemistry is choosing for them.
Why this mattersIf the true cause is dopamine, then any toy that doesn't deliver its own dopamine reward loop will fail — no matter how educational, creative, or expensive. This reframes the entire toy category: it's not about the toy's features. It's about whether the toy can match the screen's neurochemistry.
The knowledge gapMost parents blame themselves ("I should enforce limits better") or the child ("he's addicted"). They don't understand the neurochemical mechanism. Once they do, the problem shifts from personal failure to a chemistry problem with a chemistry solution.

2.2 Why Other Solutions Fail

AlternativeWhat parents believeWhy it actually fails
Taking screens away"If I just remove it, they'll find something else to do."Removing the dopamine source without providing an alternative triggers withdrawal (tantrums, meltdowns). The child doesn't have another reward source, so they demand the screen back.
Screen time limits"30 minutes a day is reasonable."Limits create a scarcity loop — the child spends the non-screen time anticipating screen time. Their attention isn't on the alternative activity. It's on the countdown.
Educational toys"If it says 'STEM' on the box, it's better than the iPad."Educational toys front-load learning and back-load reward. Screens do the opposite. A child will always choose the path of immediate reward unless the alternative provides its own.
LEGO / construction kits"Building things is creative and screen-free."LEGO has a finish line. Once built, the dopamine stops. The set goes on the shelf. Open-ended + reward loop is the missing combination.
Arts & crafts"Drawing and painting are wholesome activities."Require supervision, create mess, and the reward (a finished drawing) is delayed. Screens give rewards every 3 seconds. A 20-minute art project can't compete.
Willpower / guilt"I just need to be a better parent."You're not fighting your parenting. You're fighting a dopamine system designed by the best engineers in the world. Willpower is a finite resource competing against an infinite reward loop.

2.4 Solution Mechanism

Layer 1: The Dopamine Click

The magnetic snap sound delivers a micro-dopamine reward every time two pieces connect. This creates a self-sustaining reward loop: build → click → dopamine → build more. The child doesn't need external motivation (parental encouragement, instructions, screen prompts). The mechanism is built into the physics of the toy.

Layer 2: Open-Ended Architecture

No instructions. No finish line. No "completed" state. Unlike LEGO (build the set, done) or puzzles (solve it, shelve it), Click Blocks have infinite configurations. The dopamine loop never terminates because there's always another thing to build. The child decides the game, so the game never ends.

Layer 3: Narrative Layer (Characters)

Included characters transform construction into storytelling. The child isn't just snapping blocks — they're building worlds, creating stories, assigning roles. This engages language, imagination, and social cognition simultaneously, making the play deeper and longer-lasting than pure construction.

Proprietary mechanism name "The Dopamine Click" — a magnetic sound-reward loop that gives children the same micro-dopamine hits screens use, redirected into building, creating, and imagining instead of scrolling.

2.5 Reframe Chains

Variation 1: Logical / Educational

1. Most parents think their child prefers screens because screens are more interesting than toys.

2. But the real reason is neurochemical: screens deliver micro-dopamine rewards every few seconds. No traditional toy matches that frequency.

3. That's why every toy you've bought ends up in the cupboard. Not because the toy was bad — because it couldn't match the screen's reward chemistry.

4. Click Blocks work because every magnetic snap delivers the same dopamine micro-reward. The building loop replaces the scrolling loop — without you having to fight for it.

Variation 2: Emotional / Story-Based

1. I used to hand my kid the iPad just to get 20 minutes of peace. Every time, I'd feel that stab of guilt.

2. I'd buy toys. Nice ones. They'd sit in a cupboard after day one. Because nothing can compete with a screen.

3. Then I found out WHY. Screens aren't better than toys. They just deliver dopamine faster. And no toy I bought could match that speed.

4. Until these. Every time the pieces click together, they make this sound. And that sound gives them a tiny hit of dopamine — the same thing the screens use. Except now it's keeping them building. For hours.

Variation 3: Provocative / Pattern-Interrupt

1. You're not a bad parent. You're fighting a dopamine system designed by the best engineers in Silicon Valley.

2. Every app, every game, every social feed is engineered to deliver rewards faster than real life can. Your kid's brain isn't choosing the screen. The screen's chemistry is choosing for your kid.

3. Taking the screen away doesn't fix it. Buying "educational" toys doesn't fix it. Setting timers doesn't fix it. Because none of those solutions match the dopamine frequency.

4. One toy does. Every magnetic snap = a micro-reward. Same chemistry, different direction. Building instead of scrolling. Creating instead of consuming. And no meltdowns when you take it away — because the reward is still flowing.

Key Takeaways — Reframe

  • The reframe from "bad parenting" to "dopamine chemistry" is the most powerful belief shift in the strategy. It removes shame and introduces a solvable problem.
  • "You're not fighting your parenting. You're fighting Silicon Valley's dopamine engineers" is a line that could power an entire ad on its own.
  • The ASMR close-up of the magnetic click is the single strongest visual proof concept — it makes the mechanism tangible and audible.
  • The "toys collecting dust" objection is the most dangerous blocker. The subscription model is the structural answer; "78% still building after 3 months" is the proof answer.
  • Three reframe chains give different entry points: logical (educated parent), emotional (guilty parent), provocative (sceptical parent).

3.2 Proof Hierarchy

  1. Behavioural proof from real parents: "He sat there for two hours. TWO HOURS." Specific, named, time-bound. Most believable.
  2. Teacher / authority endorsement: "His teacher told me his focus has actually improved." Third-party, unsolicited, from a trusted source.
  3. Volume social proof: 100K+ customers. 4.1 Trustpilot. 622 reviews. Safety in numbers.
  4. Brand stats with specificity: "82% of parents report less screen time." Powerful but self-reported — needs visual support.
  5. Visual/auditory proof: The click sound itself. The timelapse of a child building for 2 hours. The creation carried to the car. Seeing is believing.

3.3 Urgency Frames (Ethical)

  • Developmental window: "Every month of unchecked screen time is a month of missed development. Start the alternative now."
  • Seasonal: "Summer sale," "Back to school," "Christmas gift guide." Genuine time-bound events.
  • Stock-based: Only if genuine. "Up to 73% off" sale pricing with an end date is sufficient urgency without manufactured scarcity.
  • Social proof momentum: "101,488 parents already switched." The bandwagon is real — use it.

3.4 Risk Removal

  • 30-day guarantee: "If your kid doesn't love them, full refund." At $24 entry, this is almost unnecessary — but it removes the last excuse.
  • Price framing: "Twenty-four bucks. Less than one month of Disney+. Less than one drive-through dinner."
  • Social normalisation: "100,000+ parents already bought these." You're not the guinea pig.
  • Confidence close: "Good luck getting them back." Stealth proof that the product delivers.

3.5 Persuasion Stack (Ad Positions)

Position 1: Guilt Activation — "I used to hand my kid the iPad"

Open with the parent's confession. They see themselves immediately. No product, no claim, just their own life reflected back. Works for cold traffic at maximum scale.

Position 2: Mechanism Reveal — "Here's WHY nothing else worked"

Pivot from problem to explanation. "It's not you, it's dopamine." The parent's shame transforms into understanding. The Dopamine Click is introduced as the answer to the chemistry problem.

Position 3: Proof Stack — "Two hours. TWO HOURS."

Behavioural proof, teacher endorsement, visual proof layered together. The parent moves from "could this work?" to "this works for people like me."

Position 4: Risk-Free Close — "Twenty-four bucks and a 30-day guarantee"

Price anchor (less than Disney+), guarantee (full refund), confidence (good luck getting them back). The only remaining objection is "I'll do it later" — urgency handles this.

Evolve

The iteration framework. What to test, what to measure, when to kill, and when to scale.

5.2 Fatigue Signals

  • Frequency > 3.0: Creative fatigue. Rotate to a new hook on the same angle.
  • CTR declining 15%+ week-over-week: The hook is dying. New creative needed.
  • CPM rising with stable CTR: Audience saturation. Expand targeting or test new ICP.
  • Comment sentiment shifting negative: Over-exposure. Pull creative temporarily and relaunch with fresh variation.

5.3 Scale Triggers

  • CPA at or below target for 3 consecutive days: Increase budget 20% daily.
  • Hook retention above 40% at 3s: The hook works. Create 3 body variations under it.
  • ROAS above 2.5× at $500+ spend: Scale hard. This is a winner.
  • Comment section generating organic UGC: Boost and reshare. The ad is creating its own social proof.

5.4 Sophistication Escalation Plan

Key Takeaways — Evolve

  • Test hooks first, angles second, formats third. Hooks are cheapest to produce and highest-impact on performance.
  • Kill fast: $200 spend or 10K impressions is enough signal. Don't let losing creative drain budget hoping it'll turn around.
  • Scale fast: when CPA is below target for 3 days, push hard. Winners have a shelf life.
  • The sophistication escalation plan (Stage 3 → 4 → 5) ensures the brand stays ahead as competitors enter the mechanism space.
  • Weekly hook rotation prevents fatigue. Monthly ICP tests prevent audience saturation. Quarterly audits prevent strategy drift.
