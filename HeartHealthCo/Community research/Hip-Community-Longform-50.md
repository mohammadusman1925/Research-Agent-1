## ANGLE 01 -- The First Three Steps After Sitting

**Concept:** Deep-buttock discomfort after prolonged sitting is one of the clearest patterns across hip-related Facebook groups. The person stands up and the first three steps feel locked, stiff, heavy -- then gradually ease. That transition window reveals something about the tissue surrounding the joint that most people overlook. *One-word: Recognition*

**Angle:** The discomfort in those first three steps is not about the joint surface. It is about tissue that has been compressed and deprived of circulation while sitting -- starved and stuck. When the viewer recognises this pattern in their own body, a routine that addresses the tissue directly becomes the logical next step.

**Use Case:** A woman, early 70s, pushes herself up from her living room recliner using both armrests. She stands still for a beat, gripping the chair. Her first step is short and cautious. By the third step, she straightens slightly. Her husband glances up from the television. She waves him off -- "I'm fine, just need a minute." She walks toward the kitchen, each step a fraction longer than the last.

**Hypothesis:** This is the single highest-frequency complaint in hip Facebook groups. Nearly every member describes this exact transition. The pattern is so specific and visual that recognition alone creates engagement. The belief shift -- it is the tissue, not the joint -- opens the product conversation naturally.

**Sources:** Sciatica / Hips & Back Pain Central group; repeated across multiple hip-pain Facebook communities as the most commonly described daily pattern.

## ANGLE 02 -- The 30-Minute Drive

**Concept:** Driving is a high-frequency trigger that competitors have not claimed. The confined seat position, the angle of the hip, the vibration of the car, and the inability to shift freely create a compression pattern that walking does not. People with hip discomfort repeatedly say a short drive can leave them worse than a long walk. *One-word: Confinement*

**Angle:** The car seat holds the hip at a fixed angle that compresses the tissue around the joint for the entire journey. Walking allows movement and circulation. Driving does not. The discomfort is not about distance -- it is about sustained compression starving the tissue. A routine that restores circulation after driving changes the equation.

**Use Case:** A woman, late 60s, pulls into the supermarket car park after a 25-minute drive. She turns the engine off but does not get out immediately. She shifts in her seat, adjusts her hip, takes a breath. When she finally opens the door and swings her legs out, she holds the door frame and stands slowly. A younger driver in the next space is already walking inside. She is still standing by her car, waiting for the stiffness to pass.

**Hypothesis:** Driving is discussed in hip communities as often as walking, but no competitor owns this trigger. The scene is universal for the demographic and immediately filmable. The fixed-angle compression framing ties directly to the starved-and-stuck mechanism.

**Sources:** Hip impingement community discussions; sitting and driving threads across Sciatica / Hips & Back Pain Central.

## ANGLE 03 -- The Side-Sleeper's Impossible Position

**Concept:** Sleep disruption is one of the highest-frequency topics in hip communities. Sleeping on the affected side creates pressure. Sleeping on the other side pulls across the hip. Sleeping on the back feels unnatural. The result is a nightly pillow arrangement -- between knees, under hips, behind the back -- that a partner cannot understand until they see it. *One-word: Exhaustion*

**Angle:** The hip does not rest when the body does. Every position compresses or stretches the tissue in a way that keeps it from recovering overnight. When the tissue around the joint cannot restore itself during sleep, each morning starts where yesterday ended. A comfort routine that supports the tissue before bed can change what the morning feels like.

**Use Case:** A woman, mid-70s, sits on the edge of the bed arranging three pillows. One goes between her knees, one behind her lower back, one wedged along her hip. Her husband is already lying down, watching. She lowers herself slowly onto her side, adjusts the knee pillow, shifts once, twice. Twenty minutes later, she is awake again, rolling to the other side, rearranging everything. The clock on the nightstand reads 1:47 AM.

**Hypothesis:** Sleep is the second-most discussed topic in hip Facebook groups after walking. The pillow arrangement is a universally recognised behaviour that creates instant identification. The emotional weight of chronic sleep loss -- and its effect on the partner -- adds depth without melodrama.

**Sources:** Hip night-pain discussion threads; referenced across multiple hip-support and chronic-pain Facebook groups.

## ANGLE 05 -- The Good-Day Flare

**Concept:** A good day is dangerous. When the hip feels better, the person walks further, does more housework, exercises a little harder, says yes to an invitation. By evening or the next morning, the discomfort is back -- often worse than before. This boom-and-bust cycle is one of the most discussed patterns in chronic pain communities, and it creates a powerful recurring use case. *One-word: Betrayal*

**Angle:** The flare after a good day is not a setback. It is the tissue around the hip telling you it was pushed past what it could recover from. The good day used up the small reserve the tissue had built. A consistent comfort routine does not prevent good days -- it helps the tissue recover from them, so one good day can lead to another instead of a crash.

**Use Case:** A woman, late 60s, has a good morning. She walks the dog an extra block. She tidies the garden for the first time in weeks. She calls her daughter and sounds upbeat. That evening, she lowers herself into the bath slowly, wincing. The next morning, she cancels a coffee date. Her daughter texts: "Thought you were feeling better?" She replies: "I was."

**Hypothesis:** The boom-bust cycle is discussed in every chronic pain community and creates deep emotional recognition. The two-word reply -- "I was" -- carries the entire emotional weight. The product as a recovery routine after good days creates a recurring use case rather than a one-time purchase rationale.

**Sources:** Chronic pain boom-and-bust cycle discussions; referenced in hip-recovery and general pain-management Facebook groups.

**Angle:** The hip does not have to be in severe discomfort for it to change how someone gets dressed. In hip Facebook groups, this is one of the most emotionally charged discussions: people who feel dismissed, unheard, and still in discomfort. *One-word: Validation*

**Angle:** The scan images the bone and the joint space. It does not show the condition of the soft tissue surrounding the joint -- the fascia, the tendons, the deep muscles that have tightened and lost circulation over months or years. The discomfort is real because the tissue is starved and stuck, even when the scan looks acceptable. Validating the experience -- and offering a routine that addresses the tissue directly -- meets this person exactly where they are.

**Use Case:** A woman, late 60s, sits in a GP waiting room holding a large brown envelope with her scan results. She is called in. She opens her phone and types into a Facebook group: "Scan came back fine. Still can't sleep on my left side. Anyone else?"

**Hypothesis:** The no-answers experience is deeply emotional and widely shared in hip communities. It creates a trust gap with the medical system that the product does not need to exploit -- only acknowledge. Validation alone generates engagement. The soft-tissue framing provides an explanation that does not contradict the medical one.

**Angle:** Choosing to explore non-invasive comfort options is not avoidance. It is a reasonable step for someone who is still weighing their choices. The tissue around the hip does not stop needing support while the person decides. A comfort routine can run alongside any timeline -- before, during, or instead of other approaches -- without replacing the conversation with a healthcare provider.

**Use Case:** A woman, early 70s, sits at her kitchen table with a printed brochure from an orthopaedic clinic. She has circled some questions in pen. Her daughter sits across from her, saying something about "sooner rather than later." The woman nods but does not commit. Later that evening, alone, she searches her phone: "hip pain relief without surgery." She is not looking for a miracle. She is looking for something to do tomorrow morning.

**Hypothesis:** Surgery fear is one of the top three discussion topics in hip communities. The key insight is that these people are not anti-medical -- they want agency in their timeline. Positioning the product as a "while you decide" routine removes the either/or framing and respects the viewer's autonomy.

## ANGLE 20 -- The Compensation Pattern

**Concept:** When the hip cannot do its job, the knee, the lower back, and the opposite hip pick up the slack. Over time, a single-joint problem becomes a multi-joint problem. In hip communities, members frequently describe knee pain or back pain that appeared after the hip started -- and the frustration of treating the wrong location for months. *One-word: Cascade*

**Angle:** The knee pain may not be a knee problem. The lower back stiffness may not be a back problem. When the tissue around the hip tightens and loses its range, the body reroutes the workload to joints that were never designed to carry it. Addressing the hip tissue directly -- where the pattern started -- can help the rest of the chain stop overcompensating.

**Use Case:** A woman, late 60s, sits in a physiotherapist's office having her knee examined. The physio asks when the knee started hurting. She thinks. "About six months after the hip." The physio nods and starts working on her hip instead. She looks confused. "I thought we were doing the knee today." The physio explains that the knee is compensating. She sits quietly, realising she has been treating the wrong joint.

**Hypothesis:** The compensation cascade is discussed frequently in hip communities and creates a powerful "aha" moment when the connection is made. It reframes existing, unexplained discomfort in a new light and positions the hip as the origin point -- which is where the product sits.

**Sources:** Compensation pattern and referred-pain discussions; multi-joint complaint threads across hip-support Facebook groups.

## ANGLE 21 -- The Weight She Did Not Plan to Gain

**Concept:** Reduced mobility leads to reduced activity. Reduced activity leads to weight gain. Weight gain increases the load on the hip. The hip responds with more discomfort. More discomfort means less activity. The cycle reinforces itself, and the person feels trapped in a body that is working against them. *One-word: Spiral*

**Angle:** The weight gain is not about willpower. It is about a hip that stopped allowing the movement the body needs to maintain itself.
