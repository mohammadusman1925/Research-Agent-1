# The copywriter bank

Where the principles come from, said in plain words, with the person who is actually responsible for them.

## The honesty rule, read this first

**Never invent a quotation.** Not to sound authoritative, not to make a point land harder, not ever.

There are two safe ways to credit someone:

1. **The principle, in plain language.** "Hopkins' rule was that a specific number gets believed and a vague claim gets ignored." This is correct and it is what you should use almost always.
2. **A line marked `[verbatim]` below.** Only the lines in this file carry that tag. Only those may go inside quote marks.

If you are not sure whether a line is really theirs, state it as a principle. Dell's own example is exactly the right shape: "from Eugene, show them, don't just tell them." That is a real principle, plainly said, correctly credited, and it is not pretending to be a quotation.

The six names below are the ones the CORE baselines in `Ad-DNA-System/00-MASTER-REUSABLE/Method/Baselines/` were built from. The mapping of each person to each pillar is this system's, not a citation from their books.

## Claude Hopkins, the specifics man

**Pillar 1, advertising is salesmanship. Pillar 5, reason why.**

His rule: an ad is a salesperson talking to one person, multiplied. It has one job, which is to sell. And a claim is only believed when it carries a specific fact. Vague superiority gets ignored.

> "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." `[verbatim, Scientific Advertising]`

He also insisted that everything be tracked. If you cannot tell which ad produced the sale, you are guessing.

**In this account.** Not one of the seven winners uses a volume number, even though the brand has 289 hip reviews, 74 shoulder reviews and claims 12,000 customers. "Loved by customers" is a platitude. "Over 12,000 Australians" is a fact. Hopkins would say the second one is the whole difference.

**Say it to Dell like this.** Numbers get believed. Adjectives get skipped. If you cannot put a number on it, it is probably not proof yet.

## Eugene Schwartz, the desire man

**Pillar 2, we channel desire. Pillar 4, meet them where they are.**

His central point is that you cannot make somebody want something. The want is already there. Your job is to take a desire that already exists and aim it at your product.

> "Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of millions of people, and focus those already existing desires onto a particular product." `[verbatim, Breakthrough Advertising]`

He also gave us the two scales every brief needs:

- **Awareness.** How much does this person already know? Unaware, Problem Aware, Solution Aware, Product Aware, Most Aware.
- **Sophistication.** How many times has this market heard a promise like yours? By the third or fourth time, the plain promise stops working and you need a new mechanism, meaning a new explanation of why it works.

**In this account.** Nobody had to be taught to want their hip to stop hurting. That want was already there. Every winning ad just points it at the belt. And because this market is at Stage 4, meaning they have heard it all before, the plain promise is not enough. That is why every winner carries the wrong depth or wrong tissue argument. It is a new reason to believe, not a new promise.

**Say it to Dell like this.** You are not creating the want. You are pointing at a want that is already there. And in a market this tired, you have to explain why this one is different, not shout that it is better.

## David Ogilvy, the respect man

**Pillar 1, and everything about headlines and images.**

His rule: the reader is intelligent. Write to them as an adult, tell the truth, and do not be clever at the expense of being clear.

> "The consumer isn't a moron; she is your wife." `[verbatim, Confessions of an Advertising Man]`

On headlines, his finding was that far more people read the headline than ever read the body copy, so the headline carries most of the weight of the whole ad. On pictures, his position was that the illustration has to earn attention and carry story, not just sit there as decoration.

**In this account.** Every single ad uses text on a plain background. Zero use the pictures the copy is already handing over: the sock she could not put on, the paperwork on the kitchen bench, the top shelf, the bra strap. Ogilvy would call that the biggest waste in the account, and the numbers agree that it is untested.

**Say it to Dell like this.** The picture is not the label on the ad. It is half the ad.

## Dan Kennedy, the match and the risk man

**Pillar 3, one piece one idea. Plus everything about the offer.**

Two things he is known for:

**Message to market match.** The right message to the wrong person fails. Same message, right person, works. So the person comes before the copy, always.

**Risk reversal.** The last thing standing between a buyer and a purchase is usually "what if this does not work for me." Answer it before they ask, and answer it in a way that costs them nothing to find out.

**In this account.** The two ads that carry the guarantee inside the story, AD 1 and AD 2, are the two most complete ads in the set. The four short ads have no guarantee at all. And the guarantee that works here does not sound like a footer. It sounds like a person: "30 day money back promise, so I figured I could send it back and feel smug about it."

**Say it to Dell like this.** Take the risk off them. And say it the way a person would say it, not the way a legal page would.

## Michael Masterson, the big idea man

**Pillar 3, one piece one idea.**

His principle: one promotion carries one big idea. Not three good ones. Stacking ideas does not add persuasive power, it splits it, because a reader can only carry one thing away.

He also framed the idea of the promise having to feel new. If your idea is one the reader has already heard and dismissed, it does not matter how well you write it.

**In this account.** AD 7 is the shortest winner and the highest volume one. It carries exactly one idea: people with this diagnosis are quietly not booking the surgery. No story, no character, no second point. It is the cleanest proof in the account that one idea beats several.

**Say it to Dell like this.** If you cannot say the ad in one sentence, it is not one ad yet. It is two.

## How to use one in a session

Pick **one**. The single most useful one for the gap in front of Dell. Not a list.

Shape it like this:

1. Name the gap in his concept.
2. Name the principle and who it comes from, in one sentence, in plain words.
3. Show it landing in one of his own ads.
4. Say what to do about it.

Four sentences is usually enough. If the lesson runs longer than a short paragraph, it is teaching two things and you should cut one.
